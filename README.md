## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard is a small web service a writing site can call to get a
cautious guess about whether a piece of text was written by a person or
generated by AI — and to let the writer push back.

1. **The text arrives** at `POST /submit` (`app.py::submit`) as JSON with
   `text` and `creator_id`. Bad input — missing or empty text, no creator, more
   than 20,000 characters, a body that isn't an object — is refused with a 4xx
   and logged as `rejected`. It never reaches the signals.
2. **Signal one** (`detector.py::model_signal`) runs GPT-2 locally and adds a
   0–1 *model score*: how predictable the words are.
3. **Signal two** (`stylometry.py::style_signal`) adds a 0–1 *style score*: how
   even the sentence lengths are and how little expressive punctuation there is.
4. **The combining rule** (`scoring.py::combine_signals`) turns the two into one
   *combined score*, 0.0 = confidently human, 1.0 = confidently AI. This is also
   the `confidence` field.
5. **The label** (`scoring.py::score_to_label`) turns that number into a guess
   (`human` / `unsure` / `ai`) and the full sentence a reader sees.
6. **The audit log** (`audit.py::log_decision`) records the decision with a new
   `content_id`, both signal scores, the combined score, the label, and status
   `decided`, as one JSON line in `logs/audit.jsonl`.
7. **The response** goes back with the `content_id`, guess, confidence, label
   and both signal scores. If the writer disagrees, they send that `content_id`
   and their reasoning to `POST /appeal`, which checks the id exists, logs the
   appeal beside the original decision, and moves the item to `under_review`.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2, running on this
machine — its perplexity, squashed onto 0–1 so that low perplexity (very
predictable) scores near 1.0. Code: `detector.py::model_signal`.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** A language model writes
by repeatedly choosing a likely next word, so its output is text a model finds
easy to predict. People wander more: odd word choices, specific details, abrupt
turns.

**What it can't see:** Who actually wrote the text. Predictability rewards
common words in short, ordinary sentences, and plenty of people write that way —
second-language writers, younger writers, anyone writing plainly on purpose. My
plain test paragraph ("My name is Ana. I moved to this city two years ago…")
scored **0.849**, almost the same as the AI paragraph (0.851). The reverse also
fails: ornate prose full of rare words reads as human, and lightly-edited AI text
scored only 0.496.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text without reading it
(`stylometry.py::style_signal`):

- **Sentence-length spread** — standard deviation of words per sentence divided
  by the mean. Even lengths read as AI-like.
- **Expressive punctuation** — marks per word from `; : — – - ( ) ! ? " ' …`,
  leaving out full stops and commas, which grammar forces on everyone. Little
  punctuation reads as AI-like.
- **Type-token ratio** (unique words / total, averaged over 50-word windows) is
  computed and printed but has **weight 0**. On my test texts it ran the
  opposite way from the theory — the AI paragraph had *more* varied vocabulary
  than any human one — so scoring it either direction would have been a guess.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends
toward even, mid-length sentences and safe punctuation, because that's what
"most likely next word" produces on average. Human writing is lumpier — a
three-word sentence next to a forty-word one, a dash, a question.

**What it can't see:** Meaning. Anything that changes the shape without changing
the substance moves the score: chopping sentences up, adding dashes or
exclamation marks, pasting in a quote. That's a cheap thing to do on purpose.
And it shares signal one's blind spot — plain writing with short, even sentences
and only full stops scores AI-like here too (the Ana paragraph: **0.824**).

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** A weighted average of the two scores — with one exception:
**if the two signals disagree by more than 0.4, the result can't be labelled
AI.** It's capped just under the AI threshold, so it lands in "unsure" at worst.
Two methods disagreeing is uncertainty, and uncertainty is not grounds to accuse
anyone. A split that leans human is left alone, because that's the cheap
mistake.

**The numbers:** (all in `config.py`)

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| `WEIGHT_MODEL_SIGNAL` | 0.5 |
| `WEIGHT_STYLE_SIGNAL` | 0.5 |
| `DISAGREEMENT_GAP` | 0.4 |
| `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` | 0.35 |
| `AI_THRESHOLD` | 0.87 (raised from 0.65 — see below) |

Inside the style signal: `style = 0.6 × spread + 0.4 × punctuation`, each
mapped linearly to 0–1 (spread: 0.10 → fully AI, 0.70 → fully human;
punctuation: 0 → fully AI, 0.08 per word → fully human). A single sentence has
no spread, so that part scores a neutral 0.5.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

Lightly-edited AI text (I took an AI paragraph and rewrote some phrases
casually, adding a dash and contractions) got model **0.496** but style
**0.761**: the edits made the words less predictable, but the sentences stayed
evenly sized. The gap (0.265) is under 0.4, so the average applies: **0.628 →
unsure**. A wider split would have been capped below 0.87 and also landed in
unsure.

**The case that set the AI threshold — both signals agreeing and wrong:**

| Test text | model | style | combined | label at 0.65 | label at 0.87 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI | 0.851 | 0.962 | 0.906 | ai | **ai** |
| Clearly human (casual) | 0.378 | 0.000 | 0.189 | human | **human** |
| Plain human ("My name is Ana…") | 0.849 | 0.824 | 0.836 | **ai ✗** | **unsure** |
| Formal human | 0.551 | 0.617 | 0.584 | unsure | **unsure** |
| Lightly-edited AI | 0.496 | 0.761 | 0.628 | unsure | **unsure** |

At the starter's 0.65, the plain human paragraph was labelled AI — a false
positive the disagreement rule can't catch, because both signals agreed. I
raised the AI threshold to 0.87, between that paragraph (0.836) and the clear AI
text (0.906). The cost: edited AI text now always gets "unsure" rather than
being caught, and the margin above the plain paragraph is thin (0.034). These
numbers come from five test texts, not a real sample.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | We think this was written by a person. Nothing in it looked machine-generated to our automated check. |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.87 | Our automated check couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or generated by AI. That happens with a lot of genuine writing, so this is not an accusation, and nothing about the piece changes because of it. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.87 – 1.00 | Our automated check thinks this was probably generated by AI. Automated checks can be wrong, including about real writers. If you wrote this yourself, you can ask for a person to review it. |

Boundaries: a score of exactly 0.35 or below is human, exactly 0.87 or above is
AI. The text lives in `scoring.py` (`LABEL_HUMAN`, `LABEL_UNSURE`, `LABEL_AI`).

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->

The people most likely to see "unsure" are people who wrote plainly — my plain
human test paragraph lands here. So it says plainly that the check failed to
decide (not that the writer is suspected), that this is common for genuine
writing, and that nothing happens to the piece because of it. It says "our
automated check" rather than "we" so the uncertainty belongs to a tool, not to a
person judging them. The AI label also names its own fallibility and points to
the appeal, because the people that label is wrong about need to know they have
a way out.

## How I Used AI

I used Claude Code (an AI coding assistant) **mainly for generating code**: it
wrote the code in `app.py`, `stylometry.py`, `scoring.py` and `config.py` at my
direction. Beyond the code, it also drafted `criteria.md` and this README for me
to review. Claude pointed out that the assignment says not to have a model
write the criteria; I chose to have it draft them as a starting point that I
revise.

**Moment 1 — setting up and understanding the project**

- *What I asked for:* To install the dependencies and run `test.py`, then to
  explain what the detector model is, what it detects, and what Milestone 1 asks.
- *What came back:* It noticed my default Python was 3.14, which the pinned
  packages don't support, and built the virtual environment with Python 3.13
  instead. It explained that GPT-2 measures predictability (perplexity), and
  that plain writing is its blind spot.
- *What I changed:* I asked for the commands in Git Bash instead of PowerShell,
  since that's the terminal I use.

**Moment 2 — building the signals, scoring and routes**

- *What I asked for:* To build `/submit`, the style signal, the combining rule,
  the labels and `/appeal`, and to suggest 10 acceptance criteria I could choose
  from.
- *What came back:* Working code, plus a calibration run on five test texts that
  showed the plain human paragraph being labelled AI at the starter's 0.65
  threshold. It raised the AI threshold to 0.87, set type-token ratio's weight
  to 0 because it ran backwards on the test texts, and changed `confidence` to
  the combined score after its first version reported "unsure" with 0.84
  confidence, which read as a contradiction.
- *What I changed:* From a list of 10 criteria that was generated by AI, I
  chose the best 5 that I can defend.
