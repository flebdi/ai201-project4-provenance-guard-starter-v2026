## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard is a small web service a writing site can call to get a
cautious guess about whether a piece of text was written by a person or
generated by AI — and to let the writer push back.

1. **The text arrives** at `POST /submit` (`app.py::submit`) as JSON with
   `text` and `creator_id`. Bad input — missing or empty text, no creator, more
   than 20,000 characters, a body that isn't an object — is refused with a 4xx
   and logged as `rejected`. It never reaches the signals.
2. **Signal one** (`detector.py::model_signal`) runs GPT-2 locally and adds a
   0–1 *model score*: how predictable the words are.
3. **Signal two** (`stylometry.py::style_signal`) adds a 0–1 *style score*: how
   even the sentence lengths are and how little expressive punctuation there is.
4. **The combining rule** (`scoring.py::combine_signals`) turns the two into one
   *combined score*, 0.0 = confidently human, 1.0 = confidently AI. This is also
   the `confidence` field.
5. **The label** (`scoring.py::score_to_label`) turns that number into a guess
   (`human` / `unsure` / `ai`) and the full sentence a reader sees.
6. **The audit log** (`audit.py::log_decision`) records the decision with a new
   `content_id`, both signal scores, the combined score, the label, and status
   `decided`, as one JSON line in `logs/audit.jsonl`.
7. **The response** goes back with the `content_id`, guess, confidence, label
   and both signal scores. If the writer disagrees, they send that `content_id`
   and their reasoning to `POST /appeal`, which checks the id exists, logs the
   appeal beside the original decision, and moves the item to `under_review`.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2, running on this
machine — its perplexity, squashed onto 0–1 so that low perplexity (very
predictable) scores near 1.0. Code: `detector.py::model_signal`.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** A language model writes
by repeatedly choosing a likely next word, so its output is text a model finds
easy to predict. People wander more: odd word choices, specific details, abrupt
turns.

**What it can't see:** Who actually wrote the text. Predictability rewards
common words in short, ordinary sentences, and plenty of people write that way —
second-language writers, younger writers, anyone writing plainly on purpose. My
plain test paragraph ("My name is Ana. I moved to this city two years ago…")
scored **0.849**, almost the same as the AI paragraph (0.851). The reverse also
fails: ornate prose full of rare words reads as human, and lightly-edited AI text
scored only 0.496.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text without reading it
(`stylometry.py::style_signal`):

- **Sentence-length spread** — standard deviation of words per sentence divided
  by the mean. Even lengths read as AI-like.
- **Expressive punctuation** — marks per word from `; : — – - ( ) ! ? " ' …`,
  leaving out full stops and commas, which grammar forces on everyone. Little
  punctuation reads as AI-like.
- **Type-token ratio** (unique words / total, averaged over 50-word windows) is
  computed and printed but has **weight 0**. On my test texts it ran the
  opposite way from the theory — the AI paragraph had *more* varied vocabulary
  than any human one — so scoring it either direction would have been a guess.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends
toward even, mid-length sentences and safe punctuation, because that's what
"most likely next word" produces on average. Human writing is lumpier — a
three-word sentence next to a forty-word one, a dash, a question.

**What it can't see:** Meaning. Anything that changes the shape without changing
the substance moves the score: chopping sentences up, adding dashes or
exclamation marks, pasting in a quote. That's a cheap thing to do on purpose.
And it shares signal one's blind spot — plain writing with short, even sentences
and only full stops scores AI-like here too (the Ana paragraph: **0.824**).

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** A weighted average of the two scores — with one exception:
**if the two signals disagree by more than 0.4, the result can't be labelled
AI.** It's capped just under the AI threshold, so it lands in "unsure" at worst.
Two methods disagreeing is uncertainty, and uncertainty is not grounds to accuse
anyone. A split that leans human is left alone, because that's the cheap
mistake.

**The numbers:** (all in `config.py`)

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| `WEIGHT_MODEL_SIGNAL` | 0.5 |
| `WEIGHT_STYLE_SIGNAL` | 0.5 |
| `DISAGREEMENT_GAP` | 0.4 |
| `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` | 0.35 |
| `AI_THRESHOLD` | 0.87 (raised from 0.65 — see below) |

Inside the style signal: `style = 0.6 × spread + 0.4 × punctuation`, each
mapped linearly to 0–1 (spread: 0.10 → fully AI, 0.70 → fully human;
punctuation: 0 → fully AI, 0.08 per word → fully human). A single sentence has
no spread, so that part scores a neutral 0.5.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

Lightly-edited AI text (I took an AI paragraph and rewrote some phrases
casually, adding a dash and contractions) got model **0.496** but style
**0.761**: the edits made the words less predictable, but the sentences stayed
evenly sized. The gap (0.265) is under 0.4, so the average applies: **0.628 →
unsure**. A wider split would have been capped below 0.87 and also landed in
unsure.

**The case that set the AI threshold — both signals agreeing and wrong:**

| Test text | model | style | combined | label at 0.65 | label at 0.87 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI | 0.851 | 0.962 | 0.906 | ai | **ai** |
| Clearly human (casual) | 0.378 | 0.000 | 0.189 | human | **human** |
| Plain human ("My name is Ana…") | 0.849 | 0.824 | 0.836 | **ai ✗** | **unsure** |
| Formal human | 0.551 | 0.617 | 0.584 | unsure | **unsure** |
| Lightly-edited AI | 0.496 | 0.761 | 0.628 | unsure | **unsure** |

At the starter's 0.65, the plain human paragraph was labelled AI — a false
positive the disagreement rule can't catch, because both signals agreed. I
raised the AI threshold to 0.87, between that paragraph (0.836) and the clear AI
text (0.906). The cost: edited AI text now always gets "unsure" rather than
being caught, and the margin above the plain paragraph is thin (0.034). These
numbers come from five test texts, not a real sample.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | We think this was written by a person. Nothing in it looked machine-generated to our automated check. |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.87 | Our automated check couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or generated by AI. That happens with a lot of genuine writing, so this is not an accusation, and nothing about the piece changes because of it. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.87 – 1.00 | Our automated check thinks this was probably generated by AI. Automated checks can be wrong, including about real writers. If you wrote this yourself, you can ask for a person to review it. |

Boundaries: a score of exactly 0.35 or below is human, exactly 0.87 or above is
AI. The text lives in `scoring.py` (`LABEL_HUMAN`, `LABEL_UNSURE`, `LABEL_AI`).

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->

The people most likely to see "unsure" are people who wrote plainly — my plain
human test paragraph lands here. So it says plainly that the check failed to
decide (not that the writer is suspected), that this is common for genuine
writing, and that nothing happens to the piece because of it. It says "our
automated check" rather than "we" so the uncertainty belongs to a tool, not to a
person judging them. The AI label also names its own fallibility and points to
the appeal, because the people that label is wrong about need to know they have
a way out.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 5 per minute, 50 per day (`config.py`, applied to `/submit` in
`app.py`)

**Why those numbers and not others:**
<!-- Think about two people: a real writer submitting their own work a few
     times an hour, and a script sending a thousand variations to map your
     thresholds. Your numbers have to be liveable for the first and hostile to
     the second. -->

A real writer submits a piece, maybe fixes a typo and submits it again, a few
times an hour at most. Five in a minute covers that burst with room to spare,
and 50 a day is roughly ten times what a busy writer would send. A script
trying to find where my 0.35 and 0.87 thresholds sit needs hundreds of slightly
different texts. At 5 a minute it gets one every 12 seconds, and at 50 a day one
identity needs weeks to map a threshold. The original 10 per minute / 200 per
day let one identity send 200 probes a day, which is enough to map a threshold
in an afternoon.

The window is a **moving** one (`strategy="moving-window"` in `app.py`). With the
default fixed window my first flood let 6 through, not 5: the first request was
slow while GPT-2 loaded, so it fell in the previous clock minute and the count
reset partway through the burst. The moving window means "5 in any 60 seconds".

**What a caller counts as:** <!-- per address, or per creator_id? They fail
differently — one script can look like a thousand callers, and one household
can look like one. -->

Each `creator_id`, falling back to the IP address when a request has no usable
`creator_id` (`app.py::rate_limit_key`). Per creator means a household or
classroom sharing one address isn't treated as one caller. The weakness: the
caller picks its own `creator_id`, so a script can rotate ids and look like a
thousand different writers, and this limit won't stop it. A real deployment
would take the creator from a login rather than the request body, and would add
a looser per-address limit on top. The limiter also counts every request,
including ones that are later refused as bad input. It runs before validation.

Each 429 also writes a `rejected` entry to the audit log with
`reason: rate_limited`, so a flood leaves a trace instead of an absence.

**Diagnoses**

<!-- For each miss AND each broken attack: the stage and the mechanism.

     Your service has five stages: the route, signal one, signal two, the
     combining rule, and the label mapping. -->

The signal values below come from running `detector.py` and `stylometry.py`
directly on the same texts. Perplexity is GPT-2's raw surprise, where lower
means more predictable.

**The pattern: one problem, not fifteen.** My combined score doesn't separate
AI text from plain human text. My 10 AI paragraphs scored 0.642–0.846. The
8 staff human texts scored 0.495–0.812. Four of the AI paragraphs score
*below* FP05. Because the groups overlap, I had to put the AI line above both
of them (0.87). The result is that nothing ordinary gets labelled AI, and any
edit that nudges either signal down only moves text further from a line it was
never going to reach. Nearly every miss below is a version of that.

**Criterion 2: missed. Stages: the label mapping, plus the combining rule
diluting signal one.**

- On signal one alone, 5 of the 10 AI paragraphs score 0.87 or above (0.872 to
  0.932). Averaging with signal two pulls every one of them under the line.
- Signal two barely tells the two groups apart. It averages 0.724 on the AI
  set and 0.685 on the human set, a gap of 0.039. Signal one's gap is 0.126.
- Part of the reason: my punctuation measure counts hyphens, and AI prose is
  full of compound words. "COVID-19", "face-to-face" and "in-office" gave the
  remote-work paragraph 0.035 marks per word, the most "human" punctuation of
  any text in criteria 1 and 2. Its style score of 0.534 dragged it to 0.642.
- The 0.87 line itself was set in unit 7 from a single AI sample that scored
  0.906. That sample turned out to be an outlier, not typical AI text.

**Criterion 3: missed. Stages: signal one and signal two, both.**

- Signal two: every staff human text has **zero** expressive punctuation (no
  dash, question mark, quote or bracket). So the punctuation part of signal two
  reads fully AI on all of them. That's 0.4 of the style score fixed at
  maximum before sentence length is even measured.
- Signal one: formal and templated human writing is predictable to GPT-2.
  FP06 (a formal letter) and FP07 (deliberate repetition) both score 0.88,
  higher than 6 of my 10 AI paragraphs.
- The combining rule can't create separation the signals don't have. My test
  material makes this worse than criterion 3 intends, since these human texts
  were written to look machine-like.

**Broken attacks.** 13 broke, from three mechanisms:

1. **Label mapping: already under the line (EV03, EV04, EV09, EV10, PI03).**
   The unedited source paragraph scores 0.775 on its own. These attacks
   lowered it a little, or not at all:
   - EV03's quotation: 0.496
   - EV04's rare words: 0.711
   - EV10's repeated stanza: 0.843, only 0.027 short of the line. Repetition
     is extremely predictable to GPT-2 (perplexity 4.1, model 0.979).
   - PI03's note "to the reviewing system": 0.539

   None needed to work for the AI text to escape.

2. **Signal two: shape-only edits (EV02, EV06, EV07, EV09).** Signal two reads
   only sentence lengths and punctuation, so cheap edits move it a long way:
   - EV02 chopped sentences: spread 0.849, style 0.180
   - EV06 one long sentence plus one short: style 0.400
   - EV07 padded punctuation: 0.083 marks per word, style 0.300
   - EV09 a human-written opening paragraph: style 0.408

   At weight 0.5, a style drop of 0.5 costs the combined score 0.25.

3. **Signal one: anything unpredictable reads as human (EV01, EV05, EV08,

## The Improvement

**What I changed:** One line pair in `config.py`. The signal weights went from
**0.5 / 0.5 to 0.8 model / 0.2 style**. Nothing else moved: same thresholds
(0.35 / 0.87), same disagreement cap, same signals, same labels. Both attack
runs report identical model and style scores for all 56 inputs. Only the
combining rule's output changed.

**Which diagnosis pointed at it:** Criteria 2 and 3, and broken-attack
mechanism 2. On the before run, signal two separated my AI paragraphs from the
human texts by 0.039. Signal one separated them by 0.126. At equal weight, the
weaker, easier-to-push signal was cancelling half of the stronger one. Five of
the evasions (EV02, EV03, EV06, EV07, EV09) moved signal two and nothing else.

I set the weights in proportion to those two separations, about 3 to 1,
rounded to 0.8 / 0.2. I did not search for the weight that passes the tests.

Before changing anything, I projected two candidates on the logged scores:

- **Raise the weight on signal one (chosen).** Projected 3/10 on criterion 2
  and a 0.100 gap: both still misses.
- **Lower the AI line to 0.82.** Projected 6/10 on criterion 2, a pass, so I
  rejected it on purpose. It would label the plain "Ana" paragraph from unit 7
  as AI, which is the false positive the 0.87 line exists to prevent. It would
  also pass FP05 by 0.008, which is fitting the line to this test set, not
  fixing anything.

## What's Still Broken

<!-- For each attack still getting through and each criterion still missed:
     what you'd do, and why you stopped. -->

I stopped at one change because this unit allows exactly one. A second change
would make it impossible to say which one moved the numbers. Here is what I'd
do next, in order:

1. **Junk scored as writing: MF03, MF04, MF05, MF06, and MF07's near-miss.**
   Stage: the route.
   - **Fix:** before either signal runs, refuse text that isn't mostly words.
     For example, require at least 3 alphabetic words, and letters making up at
     least half of the non-space characters. Return a 422 and log the refusal.
   - **Expected result:** 4 broken attacks become held, and the service stops
     accusing emoji. This would have been my second improvement. It's the
     cheapest fix with the most certain effect, which is why it comes first.
   - **Not fixed by this:** MF07. That's real writing, just not in English.
     The honest answer for it is to say "we can only check English" rather than
     score it.

2. **Confident `human` on noisy AI text: EV01, EV05, EV08, PI02.** Stages:
   signal one and the label mapping.
   - **Fix:** make the human label require agreement, the mirror of my
     disagreement cap. Today, signals more than 0.4 apart can't produce `ai`,
     but they can still produce `human`. Capping that direction too would move
     EV01 (model 0.164, style 0.880) and EV08 (0.245, 0.817) back to `unsure`.
   - **Not fixed by this:** EV05 (0.321 and 0.242) and PI02 (0.204 and 0.337),
     whose two signals agree, so they still need something else. Perplexity can't tell *unusual* from *human*. The real fix
     is a detector that compares two models' surprise rather than reading one
     raw value, which is a bigger change than this unit allows.

3. **The other evasions (EV02–EV04, EV06, EV07, EV09, PI03) and criteria 2 and
   3.** These are one problem: no threshold separates my AI paragraphs from
   plain human writing, because the signals overlap.
   - Signal two needs to stop reading hyphens in compound words as human, and
     needs a measure that does separate the groups (on these texts, nothing I
     measure does).
   - Signal one would need a stronger model than GPT-2.
   - I'd also re-run criteria 1 and 3 with **my own writing**, which the
     criteria call for and I didn't use. The staff texts are hard cases on
     purpose, so this gap of 0.100 may understate how far apart clearly human
     writing really sits. In unit 7, a casual human paragraph scored 0.189.
   - **What I would not do** is lower the AI line to make criterion 2 pass.
     The before/after projections show that buys the catch by accusing plain
     human writing.

**The trade I'd make if this ran for real:**

<!-- Two sentences. Fewer wrong accusations means more AI text getting through.
     Say which way you'd go and WHO PAYS FOR IT. -->

I'd keep the AI line high and accept that most AI text, including all but 1 of
these evasions, comes back `unsure`. That means fewer wrong accusations and a
lot more AI text getting through. The people who pay are the site and the
honest writers whose work sits next to undetected AI text. I think that's a
smaller cost than the one it avoids. A plain, second-language or non-English
writer accused by a check they have no way to argue with pays far more than a
site that misses some AI text.
